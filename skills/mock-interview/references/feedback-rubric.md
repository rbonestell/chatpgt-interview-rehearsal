# Evidence-based feedback rubric

Evaluate against the specific role and seniority. Use these dimensions where applicable:

| Dimension | Evidence to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Relevance | Directly answers the question and addresses the target competency. |
| Structure and clarity | Coherent explanation with useful detail, without unnecessary detours. |
| Ownership | Separates individual contribution from team effort without erasing collaboration. |
| Evidence and impact | Specific actions and credible results; qualitative outcomes count when metrics are unavailable. |
| Judgment and depth | Sound reasoning, tradeoffs, risk awareness, and role-appropriate technical or professional depth. |
| Reflection | Honest learning, awareness of limitations, and what would change next time. |

If scores are useful or requested, use 1–4 anchors: 1 = materially incomplete or unsupported; 2 = partial evidence with important gaps; 3 = clear, credible evidence meeting the expected level; 4 = strong evidence with depth, tradeoffs, and reflection. Mark untested dimensions N/A. Scores are coaching shorthand, not a validated hiring assessment; avoid false numerical precision or probability claims.

For behavioral answers, organize stronger key points as Situation, Task, Action, Result; include reflection when relevant. Do not force every answer into STAR. For technical questions, assess correctness, assumptions, approach, alternatives, tradeoffs, and verification. For motivation questions, assess specificity and the link between real role requirements and the user's actual interests and evidence.

Anchor each critique to an observed answer. Distinguish missing evidence from incorrect evidence. Do not demand metrics when qualitative outcomes are more appropriate or confidentiality prevents disclosure. Suggest anonymized examples. If a factual technical claim affects the assessment and you are uncertain, verify it using available authoritative sources or explicitly mark it unresolved rather than confidently penalizing the candidate.

In the overall assessment, consider the most important role requirements, not a simple average. An early-ended or short interview warrants a narrower conclusion. If evidence is too limited, report "insufficient evidence" instead of forcing an advance decision. Show progress on retries separately from the original performance.


## Calibration and evidence ledger

Use this rubric before the interview as well as during the debrief. Keep concise evidence notes for each completed question: competency, answer substance, strengths, missing or incorrect evidence, assistance, and any revision. These are observations, not hidden chain-of-thought. Share a summary if the user requests it.

Assess independently of prose polish, pedigree, confidence, similarity to the interviewer, or cultural conformity. Use stated job requirements rather than subjective "culture fit." Do not require STAR terminology, exact answer length, or numbers in every answer. Critique grammar only when requested or materially relevant to the job's communication demands.

When correcting an answer, separate substantive correctness from presentation. State why the missing point matters for this role. A stronger version should preserve the user's natural voice and factual record. Mark any missing detail as a question or placeholder. Prefer a compact outline the candidate can own over a polished fictional speech.

Do not average away a critical unmet requirement, but do not label a competency failed when it was never tested. Overall assessment should cite the two or three strongest pieces of observed evidence and the most important limitation. A pattern requires repeated evidence; otherwise call it a single observation. Employer outcomes remain unknown.
