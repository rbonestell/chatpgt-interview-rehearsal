# Role-calibrated interview design

Read with the feedback rubric before planning interview questions. These are design defaults, not claims about a specific employer's process.

## Build a compact evidence plan

Select three to five competencies from the actual responsibilities and required qualifications, prioritized for the stage. For each, retain the source basis, relevant candidate evidence, one main prompt, and observable evidence of a good answer at the stated seniority. Record whether a possible gap is missing evidence or a confirmed limitation. A five-question session cannot test everything: prioritize rather than overloading individual questions.

Use the same competency standards throughout the session. Adapt follow-ups to the answers, not the grading standard to whether you like the candidate. Track main questions separately from follow-ups and candidate Q&A. Before asking, check that the prompt has one clear task, enough context, and no unsupported premise. Do not mistake a single miss for a recurring pattern; label it an isolated example.

## Stage realism

| Stage | Main focus | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recruiter | Concise background, motivation, basic role alignment, relevant logistics | Deep specialist exams or invented hiring policies |
| Hiring manager | Ownership, delivery, decisions, collaboration, role challenges | A series of unrelated brainteasers |
| Technical or skills-based | Work sample or scenario, reasoning, constraints, verification | Trivia unless the role actually requires recall |
| Behavioral | Specific past action, outcomes, reflection | Accepting hypothetical intentions as proof of past behavior |
| Panel | Distinct stakeholder perspectives on shared competencies | Several panelists asking questions at once |
| Final round | Scope, strategy, judgment, values demonstrated through actions | Inventing an executive's beliefs or rewarding vague culture fit |

For recruiter logistics, only ask questions relevant to the stated role and jurisdiction; do not default to salary history or personal circumstances. If user-requested compensation practice needs market numbers, verify current location-specific sources or leave amounts open.

## Calibrate to experience and profession

- Early career: allow coursework, personal projects, volunteering, service work, and internships as evidence; scale expectations to the opening.
- Career change or return to work: test transferable capability and learning with neutral questions. Do not treat a career break as a character flaw or demand private reasons.
- Senior specialists: probe depth, tradeoffs, influence, and handling ambiguity.
- Managers: probe delegation, feedback, hiring judgment, execution, and developing people.
- Directors and executives: probe organizational scope, prioritization, resource allocation, business outcomes, cross-functional influence, and accountability; use only role-relevant themes.
- Nontechnical professions: use domain-appropriate scenarios such as customer conversations, operations, analysis, design critique, or service delivery. Do not assume all users are software engineers.
- When the real format includes a case, presentation, portfolio, or coding task, mirror it within host capabilities. Give essential scenario assumptions; add constraints one turn at a time. Use text for code, numbers, and diagrams while discussing reasoning in voice. Accept pseudocode when no runnable environment exists.

## Difficulty and follow-ups

Supportive: clear, narrower prompts, more thinking space, and gentle clarification. Realistic: job-relevant ambiguity and probing. Tough: harder tradeoffs, competing priorities, and skeptical follow-ups. Never introduce abuse, irrelevant stress, or false facts.

Follow up on the highest-value missing evidence: personal action, decision rationale, impact, tradeoff, or reflection. Once sufficient evidence is present, move on. A correct concise answer does not need another forced probe.

## Source discipline

Read recruiter-provided instructions when supplied and prioritize their stated interview format over generic guesses. If resume, LinkedIn, and user statements disagree on a material detail, ask a neutral clarification before using it as a premise. Minor formatting or date ambiguity need not stall preparation. Do not infer undisclosed company interview questions or claim that practice questions are actual questions used by the employer.
